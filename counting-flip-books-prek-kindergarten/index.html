Counting flip books: a hands-on counting to 10 activity for PreK and kindergarten

How counting flip books work, what they teach (one-to-one counting, number order, fine motor), and how to run them with a PreK or kindergarten class.

A flip book is one of the simplest animation tricks there is: draw a slightly different picture on each page, flip quickly, and it moves. A counting flip book adds one rule: page 1 shows 1 thing, page 2 shows 2 things, and so on to 10. Children color the pages, cut them apart, and then count to put them back in order. When they flip, the apples grow on the tree or the friends hop onto the school bus.

Why counting is the task, not the decoration

In many counting worksheets the number is printed, so a child can match "7" to "7" without counting at all. In a counting flip book the cards have no big printed number to copy. To find out which card comes next, a child has to count the objects on it. That practices:

  • One-to-one correspondence: touching and counting each object once (K.CC.B.4a).
  • Cardinality: knowing that the last number said tells how many there are (K.CC.B.4b).
  • "One more": each page has exactly one more thing than the page before (K.CC.B.4c).
  • Counting arrangements: the objects sit in a line or in two rows of five, like a ten frame, so children start to see 7 as "5 and 2" (K.CC.B.5).
  • Fine motor skills: coloring small spaces, cutting straight lines, stacking and stapling.

And because the reward is the movement, children happily recount when the animation looks wrong.

How to run it with a class

  1. Color first. Each card is a small color by number picture. Coloring can happen in a center on day one.
  2. Cut the cards apart. Trim the page border, then make the cuts along the shared dashed lines. An adult can pre-trim the border for younger children.
  3. Mix them up. This is the important part. Shuffle the stack so the order has to be rebuilt.
  4. Count to order. Children lay the cards out and count the objects on each one, placing them 1 to 10. Ask them to count first and only use the dots at the very end.
  5. Check, staple, flip. Square up the stack and look at the edge: the black dots climb like a staircase when the order is right. If one dot breaks the staircase, recount that card. Then staple along the edge and flip with a thumb.

For PreK, do steps 3 to 5 in a small group the first time. By the second book most children manage on their own.

What to say while children count

The questions you ask turn a craft into a math lesson. A few that work well at the table:

  • "How many are on this card? How do you know?" (Children explain their counting, not just the answer.)
  • "Which card has one more than this one?" (One more, without counting from 1 every time.)
  • "Can you see the 5 in this picture?" (Two rows of five help children see 7 as 5 and 2.)
  • "What do you think the next card will look like?" (Prediction keeps attention through the stack.)

Keep it light: one or two questions per child is plenty, and the flip at the end is still the best part.

Start smaller with PreK

Ten cards are a lot for a four-year-old. Each book is printed on two pages, and the first page (the cover and pages 1 to 5) makes a complete 1 to 5 mini book by itself. Start there: color five cards, count to five, staple, flip. When that feels easy, add the second page and build the full book to 10. The child who made the small book last week now wants to "finish" it, which is the best kind of motivation.

Common snags and quick fixes

  • "My cards won't flip." Paper is too floppy. Print on cardstock (65 lb or heavier) or glue the cards onto 3 x 5 index cards, square the edges on the table, and staple close to the left edge with two staples.
  • Coloring takes forever. Let children color only the counted objects and leave the background white. The animation still works.
  • Cutting is hard for some children. Pre-cut the long lines for them and let them cut only the short ones between cards. It is still good practice.
  • A child counts 7 objects as 8. Ask them to touch each object with a crayon tip as they count, or to mark each one with a tiny dot. One touch, one number.
  • Left-handed children struggle to flip. Staple on the right edge instead, so they can flip with their left thumb.

Seasonal sets keep the routine fresh

Once children know how a flip book works, a new theme is all it takes to make it exciting again. The skill and the steps stay the same, so there is nothing new to teach: in December the sleigh fills up with presents, in January marshmallows pile into the hot cocoa.

Making it work at home

Flip books suit families too. One book makes a good two-sitting activity at the kitchen table: color one day, then cut, count and staple the next. Parents can ask the same questions a teacher would. How many are on this page? How many will be on the next one? What happens if we flip backwards? Older siblings can help with cutting and stapling while the younger child does the counting.

Extension ideas

  • Tell the story. Ask children to narrate their book: "One present, then two presents..." It is counting out loud with a purpose.
  • Count backwards. Flip from the back and count down from 10.
  • Make your own. A blank template invites children to draw their own "one more" story.
  • Partner check. One child shuffles, the other orders. Swap.

Questions teachers ask

What age are counting flip books for?

Children who are learning to count objects to 10, usually ages 4 to 6 (PreK, TK and kindergarten). Older children enjoy them as a quick math craft.

How long does one flip book take?

Coloring takes one or two short sittings; cutting, ordering and stapling take about 15 minutes with an adult nearby the first time.

What if a child puts the pages in the wrong order?

The animation looks wrong when they flip, which is a natural prompt to recount. The black dots on the card edges also climb like a staircase only when the order is right.
