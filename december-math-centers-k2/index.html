December math centers for K–2: a three-week plan before winter break

A three-week December math center plan for kindergarten, 1st and 2nd grade: what to put in each tub and how to keep centers calm before winter break.

December has fewer real teaching days than it looks: assemblies, rehearsals, half days. A simple, repeatable center plan protects your math time. The plan below uses the same four centers for three weeks and changes only what is inside the tubs, so children learn the routine once and you spend your energy on teaching.

The four centers

  1. Teacher table. Small-group teaching on this week's skill. This is the only center with something new in it.
  2. Hands-on. Counters, ten frames, cubes, or a math craft that needs counting.
  3. Independent paper task. A self-checking page children can finish without help, like color by number or a mystery picture.
  4. Game or review. A partner game with cards or dice, or a page from last month's skill.

Four rotations of 12 to 15 minutes take 48 to 60 minutes. If your math block is shorter, run two rotations a day and finish the cycle the next day; the tubs stay the same either way.

Setting up the tubs once

Spend one afternoon at the start of December and the rest of the month runs on its own.

  • One tub per center, labeled with a picture. A crayon for paper tasks, dice for games, counters for hands-on. Pre-readers find their tub without help.
  • Copies for the whole week. Print every level of the week's paper task at once and file them in folders by level, so children take their own.
  • A visual rotation chart. Four columns with children's names on clothespins. Move the pins, not the tubs.
  • A "what to do when I'm done" card in every tub: check your page with the key, file it, then read a book or pick a puzzle.

How to group children

Group by what children need in the teacher-table center, and let the other three centers be mixed. That way the small group with you is targeted, while the independent centers run on leveled materials that each child chooses. Re-group every week based on what you saw in the paper tasks: a color by number page with three wrong colors tells you exactly which facts to revisit.

Mixed groups at the independent centers have a quiet benefit too: a child who is unsure how a center works can watch a classmate for a minute instead of coming to interrupt your small group.

Game center ideas that need no printing

  • Roll and cover. Roll two dice, add, and cover the sum on a 2 to 12 board. First to cover a row wins (1.OA.C.6).
  • Make-10 Go Fish. Use cards ace to 9. Instead of pairs, children ask for the card that makes 10 with one in their hand (K.OA.A.4).
  • Ten more, ten less. One child builds a number with base-ten rods; the partner says and builds ten more and ten less (1.NBT.C.5).
  • Number line race. Two children start at 0, roll a die and hop forward on a floor number line to 20. Kindergarteners count hops; 1st graders say the addition sentence.

Teach each game once at the carpet before it goes into a tub. A game children already know runs quietly; a new game turns into a stream of questions.

Week 1: counting and addition

  • Kindergarten: counting objects to 10 and reading numbers to 20. A counting flip book is ideal for the hands-on center, and a two-level color by number page (numbers 1 to 10, then addition within 10) for the paper task.
  • 1st and 2nd grade: addition fluency within 10 and 20. Start the game center with roll and cover, and use the teacher table for strategies like making 10 and doubles.

Week 3: the hundreds chart and review

The last week before break is for review, not new skills. Hundreds chart mystery pictures are perfect here: real number work, a surprise at the end, and three levels across the K–2 range (find and color for kindergarten, fill in missing numbers for 1st grade, solve clues like 40 + 10 for 2nd grade). Kindergarteners can stay on the easiest level with an adult nearby, or repeat a color by number page they already know.

Switch the game center to make-10 Go Fish and the hands-on center to a winter flip book, so the last days feel different without any new routines. For the paper task, winter pictures keep every child included and carry straight into January.

Keeping centers calm in December

  • Same tubs, new contents. Children know where everything goes, which matters more than ever when they are excited.
  • Only the crayons on the key. It cuts the time spent hunting for colors and the squabbles over the favorite red.
  • A "finished" folder in every tub. Children file their page, so there is no line at your table.
  • Answer keys in the tub. Self-checking frees you to stay with your small group.
  • A two-minute reset. Before each rotation, everyone puts lids on and pushes in chairs. The next group starts with a tidy tub.
  • Winter, not only Christmas. Mixing in winter themes keeps every child included and carries straight into January.

Checking progress without extra work

The paper tasks are your assessment. At the end of each week, flip through the "finished" folders: who still mixes up 6 and 9, who adds within 10 fluently, who is ready for within 20. Five minutes on Friday tells you how to set the teacher-table groups for the next week, and it gives you real work samples to show families at conferences in January.

Questions teachers ask

How many math centers should I run in December?

Four is plenty: one with you, one hands-on, one independent paper task, and one game or review. Fewer, well-known centers run calmer than many new ones.

How do I differentiate centers without making four versions?

Use resources that come in levels, so one tub holds the same picture or game at two or three levels. Each child takes their level.

What should I do in the last few days before break?

Switch to winter themes and review. New skills rarely stick the week before a holiday, so practice what the class already knows.
