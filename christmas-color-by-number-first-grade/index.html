Christmas color by number for 1st grade: addition and subtraction that builds fluency

How to use Christmas color by number pages in 1st grade to build addition and subtraction fluency within 20: strategies, levels, a December plan and checks.

In 1st grade, December lands at a key point for math: this is the stretch of the year when addition and subtraction within 10 should start to feel easy, and facts within 20 come into view. A good Christmas color by number page fits that moment well. It looks like a treat, but a single page holds many fact problems, and every one of them has to be solved to get the picture right.

This post is about using those pages on purpose in 1st grade: which facts to choose, how to tie the coloring to real fact strategies, and how to fit them into a busy December without losing math time. If you want the general guide to color by number in K–2, we cover that in Color by number math worksheets: how to use them well.

What 1st grade fluency looks like in December

The 1st grade standard is clear about the goal. CCSS 1.OA.C.6 asks children to add and subtract within 20 and to be fluent within 10, using strategies such as counting on, making ten, decomposing a number, using the link between addition and subtraction, and using easier known sums like doubles.

So a December page should do one of two jobs:

  • Build fluency within 10. Lots of quick facts like 6 + 3, 9 − 4 or 8 − 5, where the goal is accuracy and ease.
  • Practice strategies within 20. Facts like 8 + 6 or 15 − 7, where the goal is a good strategy, not a memorized answer yet.

Mixing both kinds on one page, without a plan, is where color by number goes wrong in 1st grade. A child who is still counting on fingers within 10 will stall on 13 − 6 and start guessing colors. Choose the level first, then the picture.

Match the page to the child, not the date

The easiest way to plan is to sort your class by what each child can do comfortably today, not by what the calendar says. Something like this works for most 1st grade classes:

Turn the coloring into strategy practice

Color by number gives children a lot of facts in a row. That repetition is useful, but it is more useful when you name the strategy before they start. Pick one strategy per page and say it out loud.

Counting on. For 3 + 8, start at 8 and count on 3. Before children color, ask them to circle any problem where the small number comes first, then turn it around in their head.

Make ten. For 8 + 6, take 2 from the 6 to make 10, then add the 4 that is left: 14. Children working within 20 can write a tiny "10 +" beside the space before writing the answer.

Doubles and near doubles. If 7 + 7 is 14, then 7 + 8 is one more. Ask children to find every double on the page first and color those spaces before anything else. It is a quick win and it shows them how many facts lean on doubles.

Think addition to subtract. For 9 − 4, ask "4 plus what makes 9?" This is the strategy that makes subtraction fluency possible, and it is easy to model with one space on the board.

A tip that works with any strategy: once the picture is done, ask "Which spaces were all the same color?" Children look back and notice that 8 + 4, 15 − 3 and 6 + 6 all made 12. That two-minute talk is where number sense grows, and it costs nothing.

A simple three-week December plan

You do not need a new resource every day. One picture can carry a whole week if you rotate how it is used.

Week 1: within 10. Use addition and subtraction within 10 at a math center or as morning work. Teach counting on and think-addition. Children who finish early color the same picture at the next level.

Week 2: move up one level. Children who were solid in week 1 move up a level (for example, from facts within 10 to facts within 20). Children who still need within 10 stay there with a new picture. Use a mixed facts page for anyone ready to switch between adding and subtracting.

Week 3: review and the last days before break. This is the week of programs and parties, so keep it calm. A page a child already knows how to do is perfect for a sub day, a shortened morning or a quiet afternoon. Send one page home as optional practice over break if your families like that.

Keeping leveled pages low-key

Leveled pages solve the biggest problem in a 1st grade room in December: a wide range of fact knowledge and children who notice who gets the "easy" sheet. When everyone colors the same picture, the finished pages look alike, so the level is hard to spot.

A few ways to keep it smooth:

  • Code the levels quietly. Use a small dot or letter in the corner instead of saying "the easy one". Children rarely compare.
  • Let children move up mid-week. If a child finishes level A quickly and correctly, the next page can be level B with the same picture. Moving up feels like a reward.
  • Give support without changing the page. A ten frame, a number line to 20 or a set of counters next to the page helps a child stay on grade-level facts.
  • Stretch your fastest finishers without more pages. Ask them to write one more problem for each color on the back. Writing a new fact that equals 12 is harder than solving one.

How to check a whole class in minutes

The colored answer key is your best friend. Lay it next to a finished page and wrong answers jump out as spaces in the wrong color. You do not need to read every problem.

For a quick fluency check, choose three spaces of different colors on each child's page and ask the child to tell you how they solved them. You will hear right away who counted on fingers, who made ten and who simply knew the fact. Jot a word or two on a class list. In five minutes you know who is ready for facts within 20 in January.

Common snags and fixes

Coloring takes longer than the math. Ask children to color only the spaces they solved today and finish the rest tomorrow.

Children skip the problems and color by shape. Have them write each answer inside the space, small, before coloring. It also gives you a record of their thinking.

Crayon chaos. Our pages list 11 crayons; put just those in a cup or bag per table and check the color key once before you start.

Small numbers are hard to read. Print at 100% on US Letter paper. Children who struggle with small spaces can work at a table with you or use a highlighter to mark the space they are working on.

Mixed facts cause errors. When a page mixes plus and minus, ask children to read each problem aloud in a whisper, sign included, before solving. Most mixed-fact mistakes are sign mistakes, not math mistakes.

Before you print

Check one row of problems on any page you plan to use and make sure the facts match what you are teaching this week. Count the crayon colors you need. Decide who gets which level. Then the page does the rest, and you get a little December calm with real fact practice behind it.

Questions teachers ask

What math should a 1st grade Christmas color by number page practice?

The facts your class is working on right now. For most 1st grade classes in December that means addition and subtraction within 10, moving toward facts within 20 (CCSS 1.OA.C.6). Pick pages that say exactly which facts they use.

How do I stop children from guessing colors instead of solving?

Ask them to write the answer small inside each space before they color, at least for the first page. Then a quick look at a few spaces tells you whether the math was done, and the colored answer key shows any wrong answers.

Can the whole class color the same picture at different levels?

Yes, if the pages come in levels. Children working on facts within 10 and children working within 20 can color the same Santa or tree, and one answer key checks every level, so no one stands out.
