Hundreds chart mystery pictures: how to use them in K–2 (and why they work)

How hundreds chart mystery pictures build number sense, how to run one picture at three levels in a K-2 class, and how to set them up as a December math center.

By PracticalShelf · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

  • When they fit best
  • Pair them with color by number

A hundreds chart mystery picture is a 10 by 10 grid of the numbers 1 to 100 with a short list of numbers to color in each crayon color. Color every listed number in the right crayon and a hidden holiday picture appears. Children stay with it because they want to see what it is, and along the way they find and read dozens of numbers up to 100. It is one of the few worksheets that children ask to finish.

What children practice on one page

The hidden picture is the reward. The math is in the searching.

  • Counting and reading numbers to 100. To color 47, a child has to find it, which means knowing that the chart counts by ones along each row and that 47 comes after 46 (K.CC.A.1, 1.NBT.A.1).
  • Place value. Quickly finding 47 means knowing that it lives in the row that starts with 41 and sits seven places along. Tens decide the row, ones decide the column. That is place value doing real work (1.NBT.B.2).
  • Patterns in the chart. Every number in a column ends in the same digit, and going down one row adds ten. Many children spot this on their own after one or two pictures. Ask them about it afterwards.
  • Ten more and ten less. At the hardest level the clues are written as 40 + 10 or 75 − 10, so children can move down or up a row instead of counting by ones (1.NBT.C.4, 1.NBT.C.5).
  • Following a key. Matching each list of numbers to its crayon is a small working-memory workout that helps with every other multi-step worksheet.

Three levels, one picture

The easiest way to differentiate is to keep the picture the same and change how children get to the numbers. Everyone sees the same picture appear, so nobody's page looks "easier".

Level What the child does Good for
A: find and color Every number is printed. Color the listed numbers. Late kindergarten, early 1st
B: fill and color About 20 numbers are missing. Write them in, then color. 1st grade
C: solve and color The list is clues like 40 + 5 or 36 + 10. Solve, then color. Late 1st, 2nd grade

If one child flies through level A, hand them level B of a picture they have not seen yet. Level B adds a real writing-numbers task without losing the surprise.

A worked example

Say the green list on a level C page starts with 30 + 4, 55 − 10 and 20 + 15. A child solves 30 + 4 = 34, finds the row from 31 to 40, counts four places along and colors 34 green. For 55 − 10 they find 55 and simply go up one row to 45, no counting back needed. For 20 + 15 they can count on, or think "two tens and one ten and five ones": 35. Three clues, three different strategies, one picture slowly appearing.

How to set it up in a math center

  1. Print in black and white. Each crayon swatch has its color word printed beside it, so you do not need a color printer. Children can color the swatches first to make their key.
  2. Put out exactly the crayons on the page. Three or four colors, not the whole box. It saves a surprising amount of time and arguing.
  3. Model one row together. Show how you find 23: "I go to the row that starts with 21, then count along." Children copy the strategy, not just the answer.
  4. Let them guess out loud. Halfway through, ask what they think the picture is. It keeps the energy up in the second half.
  5. Check with the colored key. A finished picture partly checks itself: if one square is off, the shape looks wrong and children often spot it before you do.

When they fit best

Mystery pictures are made for the weeks when attention is short and the room is loud: the last weeks before winter break, the day after a holiday, a rainy indoor recess. They make a good early-finisher choice that is still real math, and they work as December sub plans because the directions fit on one line. A page takes 15 to 30 minutes, so it slots into one center rotation or a calm start to the morning.

At home, they are a good screen-free activity for a 6 or 7 year old: one picture after dinner, with a parent reading the clues for level C if needed.

Pair them with color by number

For younger children, or on days when a 100 chart is too much, color by number pages practice the same "solve it, then color it" habit with numbers to 10 or 20. They make a good bridge from kindergarten into the hundreds chart.

Small tips that make a big difference

  • Mark row starts outside the chart. For kindergarten, put a small dot in the margin next to 1, 11, 21 and so on, so children have landmarks without coloring inside the grid.
  • Work one color at a time. Children make fewer mistakes when one color is finished before the next is started. Stop after two colors and finish the next day if needed.
  • Use a finger as a bookmark. Younger children lose their place when they look back at the list. A finger or a strip of paper under the row helps.
  • Make your own. The blank chart invites fast finishers to hide a picture for a friend and write the list of numbers. It turns into a numbers-and-writing challenge they love.
  • Talk about the pattern afterwards. "What do you notice about all the green numbers?" is a great two-minute discussion once the picture is done.

Questions teachers ask

What grade are hundreds chart mystery pictures for?

Finding and coloring printed numbers works from late kindergarten. Writing the missing numbers fits 1st grade. Solving clues like 40 + 5 or 62 - 10 before coloring fits late 1st and 2nd grade.

How long does one mystery picture take?

Usually 15 to 30 minutes, depending on the level and how many numbers are colored. Level C has more clues, so it works well split over two sittings.

What do children need?

Only the printed page and three or four crayons in the colors shown in the key. A finger or a ruler to track rows helps younger children.

Should I tell the class what the picture is?

No. The surprise is the motivation. Keep the picture names on the teacher page and let children guess as the picture appears.
